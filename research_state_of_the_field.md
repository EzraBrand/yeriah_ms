# The *Great Parchment*, Reuven Tzarfati’s Commentary, and the State of Research

**Research note — 25 September 2026**

## Executive findings

1. **The evidence supports treating *Iggeret Sippurim* as the work commented on by Reuven Tzarfati.** The translated folios already show sustained verbal correspondence between lemmata in JTS MS 2367 and the text on the Oxford Great Parchment. J. H. Chajes likewise identifies the parchment’s text as *Iggeret Sippurim* and describes the Munich copyist as combining an *ilan* containing the Epistle with a separate manuscript of Tzarfati’s commentary. A phrase in Chajes’s 2021 article about “an entirely different work” was misread in an earlier version of this report: it refers to another item in the preceding discussion, not to a supposed difference between Busi’s *Great Parchment* and the work expounded by Tzarfati.

2. **Busi’s 2004 edition remains the only printed critical edition of the work it publishes.** It contains the Hebrew text, Flavius Mithridates’ Latin translation, and an English rendering of the Latin. I found a Google Books record with limited preview/search, library records, and a publisher/foundation notice that the early volumes in the series are being reprinted, but I did **not** find a lawful, stable, open-access scan or transcription of the complete book. The introduction circulates separately; the Hebrew edition on pp. 119–191 does not appear to be freely available in full.

3. **There is now a useful online surrogate for the visual manuscript tradition, but it is not Busi’s edition.** The Ilanot Portal presents Oxford, Bodleian Library MS Hunt. Add. E as “The Great Parchment,” with a zoomable image and selectable Hebrew text. It identifies the text on the parchment as *Iggeret Sippurim*. This is an exceptionally valuable manuscript witness and visual reconstruction, but it is not a digital facsimile of Busi’s critical apparatus or edited text.

4. **Direct scholarship remains thin, but the conceptual state of the field has changed substantially.** Since 2004, the major development is Chajes’s work on *ilanot*—kabbalistic tree parchments as an iconotextual and material genre. His 2021 article and 2022 monograph relocate both “great parchment” terminology and the Oxford artifact within the history of diagrammatic Kabbalah. His 2025 conference presentation adds newly identified witnesses and a reconstruction of the parchment’s transmission. This work is more important for the project than a title-only bibliography would suggest.

5. **RAMBI confirms the scarcity of focused articles.** Exact searches for the Hebrew and English titles, Tzarfati’s name in Hebrew and English, and *Iggeret Sippurim* produced only one directly relevant indexed item: Mauro Perani’s 2005 review of Busi’s *Great Parchment* together with the following volume in the Pico series. RAMBI did not reveal a later article devoted specifically to Tzarfati’s commentary or to Busi’s text.

## 1. What exactly are the works under discussion?

Three documentary layers should be distinguished without treating them as unrelated works:

| Object | Description | Present status |
|---|---|---|
| **Reuven Tzarfati, *Perush ha-Yeri‘ah ha-Gedolah*** | A fourteenth-century Italian kabbalistic commentary. JTS MS 2367, fols. 76b–97b, is the witness being transcribed in this repository. Other witnesses or fragments are catalogued in London, Munich, Parma, and elsewhere. | Unedited as a complete critical text. The present project is producing a new transcription and translation of one witness. |
| **Anonymous *Iggeret Sippurim*** | An allusive sefirotic composition transmitted on an *ilan* and in text-only copies. Busi’s edition publishes this work under the title *The Great Parchment*. | Critically edited in 2004; also represented visually in Oxford MS Hunt. Add. E on the Ilanot Portal. |
| **The physical/visual “Great Parchment” tradition** | Large diagrammatic parchment artifacts or copies whose arrangement is integral to their meaning. The Oxford manuscript is a seventeenth-century copy of an older parchment formerly associated with Egidio da Viterbo, Catherine de’ Medici, and Isaac Casaubon. | Newly contextualized and partially reconstructed through the Ilanot Project. |

My 2019 Seforim Blog essay treated Busi’s *Iggeret Sippurim* as the underlying text of Tzarfati’s commentary. The manuscript comparison undertaken for this project supports that identification: the Oxford parchment repeatedly supplies the very clauses that JTS MS 2367 marks and expounds as lemmata. Chajes’s recent reconstruction points in the same direction. His [2025 Palermo presentation](https://prezi.com/p/ma1gjlwxbguh/palermo-2025/) calls the Epistle the narrative text of Oxford MS Hunt. Add. E and reports that the scribe of Munich, BSB Cod. hebr. 58 used both an *ilan* “for the Epistle” and a separate manuscript containing Tzarfati’s commentary to make an integrated version.

The contrary claim in an earlier version of this report resulted from misreading pp. 175 n. 48–49 of Chajes’s [“The Kabbalistic Tree as Material Text”](https://www.torrossa.com/en/resources/an/5189764). The words “an entirely different work” occur in note 48 and refer back to a different item in the preceding discussion. Note 49 then adduces Tzarfati’s *Commentary on the Great Parchment* and *Commentary on the Small Parchment* as evidence for the use of *yeri‘ah* in titles; it does not sever Tzarfati’s commentary from *Iggeret Sippurim*. Chajes’s later, more explicit account confirms the connection. The remaining editorial question is therefore not whether these are unrelated compositions, but how the several textual and visual witnesses differ and how closely each preserves the parchment that Tzarfati saw.

## 2. Baseline scholarship

### Gershom Scholem (1933–1934 / 1937)

Scholem’s catalogue of commentaries on the ten sefirot registered Tzarfati’s *Perush ha-Yeri‘ah ha-Gedolah* and *Perush ha-Yeri‘ah ha-Ketanah*. This remains the starting bibliographic notice rather than an interpretation of the work. My [2019 survey](https://seforimblog.com/2019/07/ha-yeriah-ha-gedolah/) gives the reference as *Kiryat Sefer* 10 (1933–1934), p. 504, no. 52.

### Ephraim Gottlieb (1976)

Gottlieb briefly discussed Reuven Tzarfati in *Studies in the Kabbalah Literature*, ed. Joseph Hacker (Tel Aviv, 1976). This is still one of the few studies centered on the commentator, although it does not provide an edition of the *Perush ha-Yeri‘ah ha-Gedolah*.

### Giulio Busi, Simonetta M. Bondoni, and Saverio Campanini (2004)

Giulio Busi, with Simonetta M. Bondoni and Saverio Campanini, eds., *The Great Parchment: Flavius Mithridates’ Latin Translation, the Hebrew Text, and an English Version* (Turin: Nino Aragno, 2004), 272 pp., ISBN 978-88-8419-189-2. The volume includes a general introduction, Mithridates’ Latin translation, the Hebrew *Iggeret Sippurim* (pp. 119–191), and an English version based on the Latin.

The volume is indispensable for the work it edits and for Busi’s discussion of the manuscripts and Tzarfati. It does **not**, however, publish a complete edition of Tzarfati’s commentary. Busi reported possessing a transcription of the commentary, but I have found no evidence that this transcription was subsequently published.

### Ezra Brand (2019 and later Academia paper)

- Ezra Brand, [“Towards Decoding Ha-Yeriah Ha-Gedolah (The Great Parchment), A Cryptic 14th Century Italian Kabbalistic Text”](https://seforimblog.com/2019/07/ha-yeriah-ha-gedolah/), *The Seforim Blog*, 7 July 2019. This mapped the composition’s sections and motifs, emphasized its allusive style and unusually strong interest in evil, and raised questions about its relation to Tzarfati.
- Ezra Brand, [“The Yeriah Gedolah: An Allusive Rendering of the Sefirot”](https://yeshiva.academia.edu/EzraBrand/Medieval%20Kabbalah), Academia.edu. This developed the description of the work and identified the relationship between text, commentary, and related works as an open problem.

Both pieces remain useful descriptions of the Busi text. Their identification of that text as the object of Tzarfati’s commentary is supported by the lemma correspondences now visible in the JTS and Oxford witnesses, although the exact recension and layout available to Tzarfati still require reconstruction.

### Joseph Dan (2020)

Joseph Dan, *Toldot Torat ha-Sod ha-‘Ivrit: Yemei ha-Beinayim*, vol. 13, *Be-‘Iqvot ha-Zohar B* (Jerusalem: Zalman Shazar Center, 2020), ch. 6, [“Ha-Yeri‘ah ha-Gedolah”](https://kotar.cet.ac.il/kotarapp/index/Chapter.aspx?nBookID=113680470&nTocEntryID=113681862), pp. 243–253.

Dan treats the anonymous composition as an exceptionally difficult fourteenth-century Italian work, probably dependent on secondary transmission of Zoharic material through Menahem Recanati. He attributes much of the difficulty to the loss of the original parchment and the resulting detachment of sentences from their spatial contexts. Chajes’s identification of the Oxford *ilan* and related witnesses does not erase Dan’s literary analysis, but it materially changes that premise: a historically significant visual copy and cognate witnesses can now be studied.

## 3. Work from the last twenty years, beyond Dan

### A. Directly consequential studies

#### J. H. Chajes, “The Kabbalistic Tree as Material Text” (2021)

J. H. Chajes, [“The Kabbalistic Tree as Material Text”](https://www.torrossa.com/en/resources/an/5189764), *Henoch* 43, no. 1 (2021): 162–196.

This is the most important article for reassessing the project. Chajes argues that *ilanot* are not merely texts accompanied by diagrams: their parchment-roll medium, scale, scrolling, spatial organization, and arboreal schema jointly produce meaning. The term *yeri‘ah* itself could function metonymically for such an artifact. Pages 175 n. 48–49 must be read carefully: the phrase “an entirely different work” in note 48 belongs to the preceding comparison, while note 49 cites Tzarfati’s two commentary titles as evidence for parchment terminology. The notes do not claim that Busi edited a work different from the one Tzarfati commented upon. The article’s enduring contribution here is material and methodological, not a severing of the textual genealogy.

#### J. H. Chajes, *The Kabbalistic Tree* (2022)

J. H. Chajes, [*The Kabbalistic Tree: A History of the Kabbalistic Diagram*](https://www.psupress.org/books/titles/978-0-271-09345-1.html) (University Park: Penn State University Press, 2022), especially pp. 47–51.

The book supplies the larger history of the *ilan* as a visual, material, and pedagogical genre. Its treatment of the Great Parchment tradition connects *Iggeret Sippurim* with Oxford, Bodleian Library MS Hunt. Add. E and reframes the composition as an iconotext whose layout cannot safely be reduced to a linear sequence. For future translation, its chief implication is that apparently abrupt transitions may encode movement across a diagram rather than defective prose organization.

#### J. H. Chajes, “Enigmas of the Great Parchment and the Origins of the Ilanot Genre” (2025 conference paper)

Chajes presented [“Enigmas of the Great Parchment and the Origins of the Ilanot Genre”](https://prezi.com/p/ma1gjlwxbguh/palermo-2025/) at the XXXVIII conference of the Associazione Italiana per lo Studio del Giudaismo, Palermo, 8–10 September 2025. The online presentation/transcript is the newest focused treatment I found; it is not yet a peer-reviewed article.

The presentation reports several major findings:

- Oxford MS Hunt. Add. E is a copy made by James Hepburn in 1606–1607 from an older parchment that had passed through the collections of Egidio da Viterbo and Catherine de’ Medici and was studied by Isaac Casaubon.
- the text inscribed on it is *Iggeret Sippurim*;
- Uppsala University Library MS O Hebr. 33:3 preserves another important witness associated with the Great Parchment tradition;
- a Parma sketch and the Uppsala witness help recover the composition’s visual arrangement;
- Munich, BSB Cod. hebr. 58 contains a scribal statement about using both an *ilan* and a separate Tzarfati commentary to create an integrated copy.

These claims need to be cited to a forthcoming publication when one appears, but they define the current research frontier.

### B. Closely related visual and Italian-Kabbalah studies

#### Chajes, “Kabbalistic Trees (Ilanot) in Italy” (2019)

J. H. Chajes, [“Kabbalistic Trees (Ilanot) in Italy: Visualizing the Hierarchy of the Heavens”](https://ilanot.haifa.ac.il/site/?p=585), in Giulio Busi and Silvana Greco, eds., *The Renaissance Speaks Hebrew* (Milan: Silvana Editoriale, 2019), 170–183.

This exhibition essay gives an accessible history of Italian *ilanot*. It explicitly notes that works known as Tzarfati’s “Commentary on the Great Parchment” and “Commentary on the Lesser Parchment” survive mostly as text-only copies but were copied from, and originally integrated with, diagrammatic parchments.

#### Chajes and Eliezer Baumgarten, “Visual Kabbalah in the Italian Renaissance” (2022)

J. H. Chajes and Eliezer Baumgarten, [“Visual Kabbalah in the Italian Renaissance: The Booklet of Kabbalistic Forms”](https://cris.haifa.ac.il/en/publications/visual-kabbalah-in-the-italian-renaissance-the-booklet-of-kabbali/), *The Vatican Library Review* 1, no. 1 (2022): 91–145, DOI [10.1163/27728641-00101001](https://doi.org/10.1163/27728641-00101001).

This is not a study of the Great Parchment itself, but its critical edition and analysis of a ca. 1500 Italian compilation of kabbalistic forms is directly relevant to the visual environment in which Tzarfati’s commentary and related diagrammatic traditions were transmitted.

#### Anthony Grafton and Joanna Weinberg (2011)

Anthony Grafton and Joanna Weinberg, *“I Have Always Loved the Holy Tongue”: Isaac Casaubon, the Jews, and a Forgotten Chapter in Renaissance Scholarship* (Cambridge, MA: Belknap Press of Harvard University Press, 2011), pp. 83–85.

This is a reception-history source rather than an interpretation of *Iggeret Sippurim*. It is important for the route by which the old parchment reached Casaubon and for the circumstances of Hepburn’s Oxford copy.

#### Moshe Idel (2011)

Moshe Idel, *Kabbalah in Italy, 1280–1510: A Survey* (New Haven: Yale University Press, 2011), especially pp. 148–150.

Idel provides the broader setting of Italian Kabbalah and briefly treats Reuven Tzarfati. It is contextual rather than a focused study of the commentary.

### C. Review and bibliographic reception

Mauro Perani reviewed Busi’s *Great Parchment* together with the next volume in the Pico series in *Materia Giudaica* 10, no. 2 (2005): 415–422. The [RAMBI record](https://www.nli.org.il/he/articles/RAMBI000446176/NLI) is the only directly relevant periodical item retrieved by the searches described below. A further short review is listed in the *EAJS Newsletter* 16 (2005), but I did not find evidence there of a substantive new interpretation of the work.

## 5. Is the Busi edition available online?

### What I found

- [Google Books](https://books.google.com/books?id=3d1bi-tz-kUC) has a bibliographic record, searchable snippets, a contents list, and a limited “Preview this book” interface. It does not provide dependable full-view access to the complete Hebrew text and apparatus.
- The [Fondazione Palazzo Bondoni Pastorio research page](https://www.fondazione-bondonipastorio.eu/Ricerca.html) lists the first five volumes of the series, including *The Great Parchment*, as being reprinted. It does not offer the book as an open PDF.
- Library catalogues record physical copies; for example, the [Pontifical Gregorian University catalogue](https://oseegenius.unigre.it/pug/resource?uri=991006707979707616) supplies full bibliographic data.
- I found no complete copy in Internet Archive, HathiTrust, institutional repositories, or the open web under the title/ISBN searches attempted.

### What is available instead

- [Oxford, Bodleian Library MS Hunt. Add. E on the Ilanot Portal](https://www.ilanot.org/detail?id=https%3A%2F%2Filanot.org%2Fresource%2Fitem%2Fmanuscript40rgm): image plus selectable Hebrew text for the parchment witness. This is the best open digital resource for the underlying *Iggeret Sippurim* visual artifact.
- Digitized manuscript witnesses of Tzarfati’s commentary through the National Library of Israel/Ktiv, including [JTS MS 2367](https://www.nli.org.il/he/discover/manuscripts/hebrew-manuscripts/itempage?docId=PNX_MANUSCRIPTS990001050100205171&scope=PNX_MANUSCRIPTS&vid=MANUSCRIPTS), [British Library Add. 27078](https://www.nli.org.il/he/discover/manuscripts/hebrew-manuscripts/itempage?docId=PNX_MANUSCRIPTS990000640890205171&scope=PNX_MANUSCRIPTS&vid=MANUSCRIPTS), and [Parma, Cod. Parm. 2419](https://www.nli.org.il/en/discover/manuscripts/hebrew-manuscripts/itempage?docId=PNX_MANUSCRIPTS990000710590205171&vid=MANUSCRIPTS).
- Busi’s introduction circulates on Academia.edu, but the existence of the introduction online should not be confused with access to the complete edition.

**Bottom line:** I did not locate the full Busi edition online in a lawful, stable, open form. The Ilanot Portal now supplies something arguably more revealing for the work’s visual form, but it does not replace Busi’s edition for textual variants, Latin translation, English rendering, or scholarly apparatus.

The labels attached to some catalogue records are inconsistent, and “Great Parchment” can denote a title, a physical format, or a genre. Shelfmark-level verification should therefore precede any stemma or identification claim.

## 7. Current state of the field

The research history can be summarized as follows:

1. **Catalogue phase:** Scholem registered Tzarfati’s commentaries.
2. **Author/context phase:** Gottlieb and later Idel placed Tzarfati within Italian Kabbalah.
3. **Edition phase:** Busi, Bondoni, and Campanini published *Iggeret Sippurim*, Mithridates’ Latin, and an English rendering under the title *The Great Parchment*.
4. **Interpretive phase:** Brand and Dan described the anonymous work’s literary and theological features, still working largely from the printed linear text.
5. **Material-visual phase:** Chajes reclassified the relevant artifacts as *ilanot*, recovered visual witnesses and transmission history, and clarified how *Iggeret Sippurim* and Tzarfati’s separately transmitted commentary could be recombined in an integrated copy.

The field’s chief lacuna is no longer merely “this text is obscure.” It is a more precise set of unanswered questions:

- Which recension and visual arrangement of the Great Parchment did Tzarfati comment on?
- How completely can its lemmata be aligned with the surviving *ilan* witnesses?
- How did copyists transform a spatial commentary into a linear codex text?
- Which differences among Oxford, Busi’s edited witnesses, and the commentary’s lemmata reflect recension, copying, or layout?
- Which readings in the commentary make sense only when restored to a diagrammatic position?
- How do the JTS, London, Munich, and Parma witnesses differ in their retention or suppression of spatial cues?

## 8. Implications for the present transcription and translation

The current project is unusually well placed to answer the most important open question, because a complete, searchable transcription of the JTS commentary can be compared computationally with Busi’s Hebrew text and with the Hebrew text encoded in the Ilanot Portal.

Recommended next stages:

1. **Use *Iggeret Sippurim* as the underlying commented text without conflating its witnesses.** Preserve the JTS manuscript’s quoted lemmata and report consequential Oxford or Busi variants rather than silently normalizing them.
2. **Extract every apparent lemma or citation marker** in the JTS commentary and build a concordance against Busi pp. 119–191.
3. **Acquire or borrow a physical/scan copy of the Busi volume** for controlled comparison, since the Google Books preview is inadequate for scholarly collation.
4. **Export or transcribe the Ilanot Portal’s encoded Hebrew** and compare it independently with both Busi and the commentary.
5. **Collate at least one additional commentary witness**, preferably British Library Add. 27078, before regularizing difficult passages in the English translation.
6. **Track spatial vocabulary and deictics**—“above,” “below,” “right,” “left,” “this line,” circles, paths, gates, crowns, and similar terms—as possible remnants of diagram navigation.
7. **Treat the commentary as an iconotextual reading protocol**, not only as continuous exposition. The layout may be evidence rather than ornament.

## 10. Search limits

This note is based on direct searches of RAMBI, the National Library of Israel/Ktiv catalogue, publisher and library catalogues, Google Books, the Ilanot Project/Portal, author publication pages, and open-web title/citation searches as of 25 September 2026. It distinguishes material I could verify from items merely cited by later scholarship. Paywalled full texts and unindexed dissertations may contain additional passing discussions. The strongest negative conclusion warranted by the evidence is therefore: **no additional focused article on Tzarfati’s commentary or Busi’s *Iggeret Sippurim* was located**, not that no such discussion can possibly exist.
